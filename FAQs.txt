Line loss - Frequently asked questions
=======================================

Q: How and why are we separating transmission and distribution networks?
A: Why: the goal of this mod is to add grid management inspired decision making to Factorio.
   Optimising production is fun, but the vanilla way of dealing with the power network breaks my
   immersion. I think there is a small niche of players that feel the same!
   Simulating electricity flow is outside the scope of vanilla Factorio, and the game engine is
   not built to handle a full DC power flow simulation over all the poles that are normally placed
   in a base. As I'd like to include some grid management mechanics, though, like dealing with
   electric congestion, power flow simulation is necessary to a degree.
   To do this, I decided to build on top of the great mod Power Overload, which already invites
   the player to separate distribution and transmission networks, although it doesn't explicitly
   call them this way. In such an environment, it's much easier to apply a proper electricity flow
   simulation, limited to the transmission network. On the other hand, distribution networks are
   handled with a simplified model where each pole is paired with the closest generator or
   transformer, which supplies energy including some loss contributions (line loss, transformer
   core loss, and transformer inefficiency). To increase the realism of such a model, the player
   can opt in to the "machine weighting" option, which steps up the complexity a notch and
   dispatches energy non-uniformly across the distribution network, according to machine live
   power demand. In such a model, a distribution network including a cluster of power hungry
   machines has less line loss if fed by a transformer close to the power hungry machines.
   Multiple transformers and local power generation can be used too to find the best balance to
   minimize losses!
   How: every pole Power Overload rates like a big electric pole or more (300 MW by default) is a
   transmission pole; every other pole is a distribution pole. A network made only of
   transmission poles is a transmission network. A network with any distribution pole or
   generator is a distribution network, and its transmission poles count as distribution poles.
   Opening the electricity overview shows which kind its network is.

Q: What is a Kirchhoff solve? Is it more realistic than vanilla, and how can its realism be pushed
   further?
A: A Kirchhoff solve is a DC power flow: power plays the current, and each wire's
   resistance-weighted length plays its resistance. Power entering and leaving the network
   (transformer outputs and inputs, machine loads) sets up a linear system whose solution gives
   every pole a potential; the power on each wire is the potential difference divided by its
   resistance. Power therefore splits over parallel lines in inverse proportion to their
   resistance, as in a real grid.
   Vanilla has no losses at all: a network is one pool, and where power flows does not matter.
   This is more realistic: routes, parallel lines and bottlenecks all change what is lost.
   It could be pushed further: an AC power flow (voltages, phase angles, reactive power), losses
   from the actual current rather than a load-based share, voltage drop at the end of long lines,
   and a full solve of distribution networks too. It is not worth pushing further for now: it
   costs much more script time, Factorio has no voltages or reactive power to show for it, and
   the gameplay would barely change.

Q: How exactly does the distribution network work?
A: Sources are the transformers feeding the network and the poles that pick up generators.
   A tree search (shortest paths from every source at once, by resistance-weighted distance along
   copper wires) finds each pole's nearest sources. Each pole's load then goes whole to its
   nearest source:
     - its nearest transformer, or
     - its nearest generators, while they have output left (solar panels with what the daylight
       gives).
   A pole is never split between sources; equally near sources take turns. A transformer drawing
   from the network takes from its nearest generators first, then from its nearest transformer.
   Power travels to each pole along its shortest path.
   How much load each pole carries depends on "Weight machines by their live load in distribution
   networks":
     Off (default): the network's machine load is spread evenly over all its distribution poles;
       each pole's share travels to it along its shortest path. This means a pole next to a
       transformer inputting power into the distribution network carries more power than one at
       the edge of the network.
     On: each pole carries the machines in its supply area, weighted by their live power draw; a
       machine covered by two poles counts half on each. Each pole's share still travels to it
       along its shortest path, so power hungry machines far from transformers cost more, idle
       ones almost nothing. Costs an entity search per pole when the network changes.

Q: How are losses worked out?
A: T = transformer. Each "->" line is the hidden consumer that draws that loss, and the network it
   draws from.

   Steam engines
        |
        v
   Generator network
        |    line loss on the wires to T gen, per tile x distance
        |    -> T gen's "Line loss", on the generator network
        v
   T gen
        |    inefficiency + core loss
        |    -> T gen's "Transformer losses", on the generator network
        v
   Transmission network
        |    line loss, grows with power; power spreads over the whole
        |    network by pole resistance (Kirchhoff solve)
        |    -> T gen's "Line loss", on the transmission network
        v
   T distr
        |    inefficiency + core loss
        |    -> T distr's "Transformer losses", on the transmission network
        v
   Distribution network
        |    line loss, per tile x distance to each pole's nearest source
        |    -> T distr's "Line loss", on the distribution network
        v
   Machines

Q: What resistance are we assigning to each pole?
A: Each pole has a resistance per tile of wire, from its Power Overload rating:
     r = sqrt(300 MW / pole rating)
   With Power Overload's default ratings:
     Pole                        Rating    Resistance
     Huge electric pole          3 GW      0.32
     Big electric pole           300 MW    1
     Substation                  125 MW    1.55
     Compact substation          125 MW    1.55
     Medium electric pole        60 MW     2.24
     Small electric pole         10 MW     5.48
   Fuses count as their pole. Other mods' poles use the same formula with their Power Overload
   rating. A wire between poles A and B costs length x (r_A + r_B) / 2. Each pole's tooltip
   shows its "Line resistance".

Q: How can I lower line loss in a transmission network?
A: - Keep wires lightly loaded: the share a wire loses grows with the power it carries. 100 tiles
     of big poles lose 0.55% at 10 MW, 2.5% at 75 MW and 9.25% at 300 MW. The "Line loading
     (% of rating)" view shows yellow above 60% and red above 80%.
   - Build parallel lines: power splits between them, and two lines sharing a load lose about half
     of what one line carrying it all would.
   - Bypass bottlenecks: in one test layout, a bypass around a single busy line cut the feeding
     transformer's loss from 9.4% to 6.9%.
   - Use huge poles for bulk power: a third of a big pole's resistance and ten times its rating.
     100 tiles of huge poles lose 0.36% at 300 MW.
   - Keep routes short, and step up close to the generators.
   - Keep distribution poles out: a single medium pole wired into a transmission line turns the
     whole network into distribution.
   - Use the "Line losses" view and the network panel's list of the wires losing the most power to
     find the best upgrades.

Q: How can I lower line loss in a distribution network?
A: - Put the transformer in the middle of the load: a 9 x 9 block of medium poles 7 tiles apart,
     40.5 MW of machines, loses 3.3% fed at a corner and 1.9% fed at the centre.
   - Feed large networks from several sides: the same block fed from two opposite corners loses
     2.1%.
   - Use lower-resistance poles for trunks: 100 tiles lose 3.9% on substations or compact
     substations, 5.6% on medium poles and 13.7% on small poles.
   - Keep distribution networks small: step down close to the consumers instead of running long
     distribution lines. Every extra transformer costs inefficiency and core loss, though.
   - Put local generation (e.g. solar) next to hungry machines far from the transformers.
   - With machine weighting on, keep power hungry machines close to the transformers.
   - Use the "Networks" view to see which transformer feeds each wire.

Q: Are there new map modes to visualise the Line loss model?
A: Four shortcuts, one view at a time, all drawn on the map:
     Line loading: power on each wire, green to red up to the busiest.
     Line loading (% of rating): power on each wire against its weaker pole's rating, yellow
       above 60%, red above 80%.
     Line losses: power lost on each wire, green to red across all wires on the surface.
       Diamonds show each transformer's total upstream loss. Opens the network panel: the network
       downstream of the transformer you hover or open, its sources, its consumption and the wires
       losing the most power, each with a Show button.
     Networks: transmission networks in blue; in distribution networks, each wire in the colour
       of the transformer feeding it, grey where several sources meet.
   In any view, hovering a transformer shows what feeds it, with each source's share and line
   loss. Wires straight from a transmission pole to a distribution pole are dashed.

Q: What is not modelled?
A: - Inside a distribution network, power from a source follows its shortest path; loops do not
     share the load.
   - Machines are not placed exactly: with machine weighting off their load is spread evenly over
     the poles; with it on, each machine counts at the poles covering it, with the average draw
     of its machine type.
   - Power from generators that reaches machines without passing a transformer is not charged:
     losses are always paid by a transformer.
   - Voltages, reactive power and voltage drop (see the Kirchhoff question).
   - Power Overload's high- and low-priority transformers are hidden.

Q: What is core loss?
A: A constant loss of a switched-on transformer, whatever it carries, as in a real transformer,
   whose core uses energy even with no load. It is 0.1% (map setting "Transformer core loss") of
   the transformer's rating, which is the rating of the strongest pole on its weaker side. It is
   drawn with the inefficiency by the transformer's "Transformer losses" consumer, on its upstream
   network. An oversized or idle transformer wastes power this way: size transformers to their
   load, and switch off the ones you don't need. A future version will add circuit network
   support, to help turn off inactive transformers automatically.
